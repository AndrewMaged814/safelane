Every change gets the rollout it deserves.

The first open-source tool that compiles a risk score into real rollout parameters for Argo Rollouts. Score the change before it ships; let the score decide how carefully it ships.

Track 1 — automate deployment & operations 15 working days to the demo 2 people Blocked on: Argo running on Ahmed's machine

The idea

Most teams ship every change the same way, however dangerous it is.

A one-word copy fix and a change to the payment retry logic get the identical rollout. The dangerous one is only caught after it is already hurting people.

SafeLane reads the change before it ships and asks one question: how risky is this specific change? It answers from what the change actually touches — how big it is, which services it hits, whether it is config or code, how hard it would be to undo, whether those files have caused incidents before, and when it is shipping. Then it puts the change in the lane it deserves, and hands the actual release to Argo Rollouts.

Two halves of this already exist separately. Tools that score risk, and tools that run careful rollouts. What nothing open-source does is let the score set the rollout's numbers.

The two lanes

The whole idea, on one screen.

Five copies of the service are running. The lane decides how many of them get the new version, and how long anyone waits before trusting it.

Fast lane · safe change ships · stays green
one step Straight to all five. No pause, no analysis, minimal friction.
Guarded lane · risky change fails at step two · rolled back automatically
20% One pod of five carries the new version. Error rate watched every 10 seconds.
40% Error rate breaks the 1% threshold. One failed measurement is enough.
abort Argo takes the canary to zero and restores all five stable pods. 30–45 seconds, no human involved.
Tier Lane Steps, as weight and pods Error threshold Check every
safe fast 100% — all 5 at once Argo health only —
guarded guarded 40% (2) → 100% (5) 3% 10s
risky guarded 20% (1) → 40% (2) → 60% (3) 1% 10s

Low confidence in a score always routes to the guarded lane, whatever the number says. A missing or invalid decision is treated as risky. Being wrong sends a change to a slower rollout, never a faster one.

Honest limit — say this before a judge finds it

These are pod counts, not true traffic percentages.

With no service mesh or ingress controller, Argo Rollouts does not split traffic by percentage — it approximates the weight using replica counts. At five replicas, weights of 1, 5, 10 and 20 all produce exactly one canary pod. So "1%" was never achievable on a laptop; it would need a hundred replicas.

Which is why the ladder is 20 → 40 → 60. At five pods those are the only values that are literally true, and every step visibly moves a pod.

The sentence for the room: "On a laptop with no service mesh, Argo approximates weight with replica counts — so this is one pod in five, not one percent. With ingress-nginx or Istio the same policy emits real traffic weights. That's a configuration line, not a redesign."

Saying it yourself turns the weakest point in the demo into evidence that you understand your own tooling. A second limit worth owning: if the injected fault fails nearly every request, the 1% and 3% thresholds trip identically — so the tier difference is real in the file but invisible on screen. Do not claim it is demonstrated.

Component and data flow

Render the manifest. Never patch it.

Patching a Rollout mid-flight resets the step index to zero, and patching one that has already aborted clears the abort and resumes toward the broken version — destroying the one thing the demo exists to prove. Render the whole file from a template, lint it, apply it once. Never mix apply with set image; they silently undo each other.

Burn a warm-up release first.

Argo skips all canary steps on a service's very first deploy and goes straight to 100%. So the demo needs one throwaway revision applied during setup, off camera. Miss this and the risky change ships instantly in front of the judges.

The risky-change path, live

The two beats

  • Trivial change, about 45 seconds. Score 8, tier safe, one reason. Straight to 100%, stays green.
  • Risky change, about 3 minutes. Score 72, tier risky, three reasons — read all three out loud, this is the money shot. Then show the two rendered manifests side by side: same service, same template, different lane. That is the entire idea on one screen.

Escape hatch if the analysis stalls: aborting by hand still tells the rollback story. The backup recording exists precisely so a bad network never costs the demo.

The interface is the plan's best decision

One file, frozen early. Every step in it carries both the percentage and the honest pod count, side by side, so the two can never drift apart. It records whether weights are real or approximated, so nobody reading it has to guess. And the default-safe posture lives in the schema, not just in the pitch: low confidence routes guarded, a missing decision is treated as risky.

Sequence, not calendar

Three phases, riskiest work first.

Impact

Lead with how well the scoring aims, not with a claim about the world.

The demo runs on seeded history and says so. Which means "failure rate went down" is not a claim seeded data can support — a judge would be right to push back.

We slowed down X% of changes, and that caught Y% of the failures.

That is a property of the policy itself, not an assertion about the world, so seeded history can demonstrate it honestly. Reported against a control group of changes deliberately left unscored.

Alongside it, the number that makes the point viscerally: how many requests were served the broken version before the rollback fired, with SafeLane and without. That is the real argument — a fast rollout hurts more people before the brake catches, and some failures never trip a simple error check at all.

DORA metrics — change failure rate and mean time to recovery, split by tier — stay as the secondary view, since the evaluation criteria name them.

Prior art — name these first, unprompted

Five neighbours, and exactly where each one stops.

A pitch that names its own nearest neighbours cannot be accused of not having looked. Every one of these turns risk into a path choice — approve or hold, gate or don't. None turns it into traffic weights, step counts and thresholds.

WhoWhat they doWhere they stop
Meta
Diff Risk Score		 Scores every change before it ships, and uses the score to pick one of four release-gating levels — in production. Picks whether and when to gate. Not how much exposure. And it only exists inside Meta.
ServiceNow
commercial		 Machine-learned change-risk prediction. Auto-approves low-risk changes, routes risky ones to manual sign-off. The approval layer — who signs off, how many gates. It never touches traffic.
Digital.ai Release
commercial		 Sells "a fast-lane for low-risk software release changes" — their words, nearly our tagline. Fewer approval gates. Nothing found says it alters rollout mechanics — say the marketing is ambiguous, don't assert either way.
Akuity / Kargo
re-check		 Scores risk from the diff plus deployment history, inside a GitOps promotion tool. Published days before we started. Advisory. It tells a human. It does not change canary weights or analysis strictness. The row most likely to move before the assessment.
DeployWhisper
MIT		 The best open-source pre-deploy scorer: blast radius, incident matching, rollback complexity, evidence trails. Refuses to act, by design — exits zero whatever the verdict, no rollout code at all. Literally the other half of SafeLane.

"Meta published this. Dell patented it. What's left?"

Granularity and availability. They choose whether to gate; we choose how much exposure — weights, steps, pauses, thresholds. And none of it is usable without a big company's internal platform. Don't claim you invented the idea. Claim the first open, Argo-native implementation, and cite Meta yourself.

"Isn't this a YAML generator plus a policy file?"

Mechanically, yes. That is exactly what it is. The value is one policy artifact instead of fifty hand-tuned canary files, plus a back-test that tells you when the policy is wrong. Conceding this is stronger than denying it.

Risk

What breaks, and what we already decided to do about it.

An empty metrics query — the most likely on-stage failure

If Prometheus returns nothing, Argo treats it as a broken monitoring setup, not as a failing app. The rollout still stops, but for the wrong reason, and the screen reads "no data returned from the metric provider." A demo that technically worked, showing a message that says your monitoring is broken.

Three fresh-cluster conditions make it likely: Prometheus scrapes once a minute by default, the error-rate maths needs at least two samples, and the canary-identifying label must be relabelled or the query is always empty.

Mitigations, all mandatory: scrape every 5 seconds, make an empty result fall back to zero, guard on result length, relabel the canary label, and run a check script during setup that fails loudly if metrics aren't flowing. One free win: configure only the failure condition and never the success condition — that makes the indefinite "inconclusive" hang structurally impossible.

Cut-line: if error-rate analysis isn't reliably green by the end of phase 1, abort on pod readiness instead. You lose "we watch the error rate" and keep "it rolls back automatically" — and it deletes the largest technical surface in the project.

The rollback that un-happens

Already designed out: render and apply, never patch. Worth re-reading before the demo, because it is the kind of shortcut that looks harmless while writing a script at 1am.

Someone in the room knows about the pod-count approximation

Roughly even odds a judge names it. Costs nothing if you say it first, and makes every other claim suspect if they say it first. It is in the deck, in the abstract, and in the contract.

Never cut, at any point

The decision file and the policy — that is the idea. The automatic rollback — that is the proof. The backup recording — that is the insurance. The honest-limitations slide — that is the difference between a demo and a claim.

Deliberately not building

The out-of-scope list, so nobody quietly re-adds it.

  • Any service mesh — Istio, Linkerd, ambient mode. If fine-grained weights are needed it is ingress-nginx or nothing.
  • More than one Rollout object, and more than one demo service.
  • Argo CD. This is Argo Rollouts — a different product, and only one of them is needed.
  • Multi-cluster, authentication, real incident ingestion, infrastructure-as-code parsing.
  • Any machine learning or trained model. The policy is deterministic and versioned.
  • Any LLM in the decision path. Turn every model off and SafeLane behaves identically.
  • The back-test loop stays a stretch goal. Until it exists it is a seeded chart, and we say so.

One idea worth saying and not building: deriving the canary size from the error budget rather than a fixed number. It is the strongest possible answer to "who picked your thresholds?" — and it needs plumbing that does not fit. Say it as the next step; spend zero days on it.
